Why I practise law

I became a lawyer because I kept noticing how often people lose not because they’re wrong, but because they can’t read the paper in front of them. So I work on the paper. I draft it plainly, I track what usually goes untracked, and I give time to people who can’t pay for it.

I’m still building, in Nigeria, toward a practice where a good agreement is the first form of justice, and the opportunity to thrive.

I am committed to using law, advocacy, research, and community engagement to help build a more just and inclusive society.

About me

Law, in service of something bigger

My journey into law has always been connected to something bigger than the profession itself: a desire to stand up for people, protect human dignity, and contribute to a society where justice is accessible to all.

I have developed a strong foundation in legal research, advocacy, analytical thinking, and understanding the systems that shape people’s lives. Beyond the courtroom and legal practice, I have always been drawn to the humanitarian sector, particularly work that addresses inequality, protects vulnerable populations, and empowers underserved communities.

I believe the law should be more than a system of rules. It should be a tool for protecting dignity, advancing equality, and creating opportunities for people to live better lives.

My experiences in legal aid, community engagement, and development-focused work have strengthened my belief that meaningful change requires more than good intentions. It requires knowledge, compassion, collaboration, and action.

I aspire to contribute to initiatives that bridge the gap between justice and human welfare, particularly through human rights advocacy, humanitarian programming, policy research, youth empowerment, and community development.

“I want my work to matter in the lives of people who need to be seen, heard, and supported.” Nenrit Angel Lambert

On dignity and legal protection

Freedom should never depend on an employer’s permission

I became involved in supporting a woman who had allegedly been unlawfully restricted in her freedom by her employer. Beyond the situation itself, she faced the barriers that so many people meet when they try to reach the justice system: not knowing where to turn, who to trust, or whether anyone would listen.

Standing with her reminded me why I chose this profession. Every person is entitled to their liberty and to the protection of the law, regardless of their income, their job, or who they work for. Access to justice is not a courtesy. It is a right, and it often begins with one person refusing to let someone face the system alone.

On compassion and community

When one voice becomes many

I came to know of a mother living with poverty and mental health challenges while caring for her baby. She was carrying far more than anyone should have to carry alone.

I used what I had, my voice and my online community, to raise awareness of her situation and to organise fundraising support. The aim was simple: turn one person’s attention into many people’s support.

It confirmed something I hold onto: individual action matters. Advocacy is not only what happens in institutions. Sometimes it is simply making sure someone is seen, and inviting others to act with you.
